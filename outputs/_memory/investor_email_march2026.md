## The IISc SLS Machine

We built India's first indigenous SLS 3D printer with IISc, funded by the Ministry of Heavy Industries, and launched it at IMTEX 2025. The machine works. The collaboration does not. Post-launch, IISc has shown no interest in commercialisation — they won't even run the demonstration parts we need for customers. The Ministry milestones are met, and with that, the institutional motivation has evaporated.

The strategic takeaway: we proved we can build a manufacturing-grade SLS system from scratch — BOM of ₹50–85 lakhs — with minimal external support. The expertise to build more of these machines lies entirely with Fracktal. That's a capability very few teams in India have demonstrated.

This matters because SLS is the production technology behind both directions I'll describe below. For surgical planning and patient-specific implants, SLS in PEEK and biocompatible polymers is the manufacturing process. For the digital warehouse, SLS produces end-use polymer parts at volumes where injection molding is uneconomic. Most companies pursuing either of these paths import their SLS machines from EOS, 3D Systems, or Farsoon at ₹1–3 crore per unit. We build ours. That vertical integration — controlling the machine, the materials, and the process — is a structural cost and capability advantage that's very difficult to replicate.

## What Has Worked

The IISc project consumed disproportionate bandwidth this year, but we opened some important doors.

We began working with ISRO's U R Rao Satellite Centre on production parts for satellite programs. Early, but real — space-grade applications are serious validation.

On defence: ₹75 lakhs in Army orders executed this year, ₹50 lakhs in pipeline. We've also developed and deployed a pellet extruder 3D printer at 515 Base Army Workshop at Trinity — the unit is already in active use, producing housings for landmines and other ordnance applications. A second Army workshop has placed an order for the same system, with installation expected by end of this quarter. This is significant: defence units are not just buying our machines — they're integrating them into live production workflows for mission-critical applications.

Through Commander Debesh — introduced by Ragu Sir — we're working with the Navy's Indigenization Unit on reverse-engineering and locally manufacturing legacy parts for Russian-origin submarines. Relationships established at Naval Dockyard Vizag, engagements begun at Karwar. This work combines manufacturing, electronics, and reverse engineering well beyond 3D printing. We bid on a ₹1 crore Navy tender earlier this year and lost — a bruising experience, but a deeply instructive one. We've come away with a much clearer understanding of how defence procurement evaluation works from the inside, and we're substantially better positioned for upcoming tender cycles. I'd also like to formally bring Commander Debesh onto our advisory board — his Navy network runs deep — and would appreciate the board's support.

Revenue for the year: approximately ₹2 crore. Below target, partly due to IISc. Defence sales cycles are long, and chasing payments has put real strain on working capital. More immediately, we have approximately ₹1 crore in active pipeline — defence orders in the ₹50 lakh to ₹1 crore range, plus Navy tenders we're bidding on — but we don't have the working capital to finance execution. Government contracts come with long cash conversion cycles: 90–180 days from delivery to payment is standard, and the upfront material and manufacturing costs are substantial. We're winning real orders from serious customers, and then struggling to fulfil them. This is a solvable problem — working capital financing, a credit line, or a small bridge — but it needs to be solved. Every order we can't fulfil is credibility we can't afford to lose with customers who took a chance on a small company.

## The Hardware Market Has Changed

Bambu Lab — backed by billions in Chinese manufacturing capital — is flooding the Indian market. Our sub-₹10 lakh segment has become unwinnable without a production-scale capital infusion we don't have. Sales cycles have lengthened. Despite Make in India protections, traders are importing partially knocked-down kits that circumvent GeM safeguards designed for OEMs like us, while flooding social media with sponsored advertising at a scale we cannot match.

We are seriously considering exiting this segment. We'll continue selling specialised machines in the ₹10–50 lakh range — for medical, defence, and aerospace industrial applications — but the commodity hardware business is no longer viable.

Bambu Lab is a symptom. The deeper problem is structural.

## The Mistake We Made

For thirteen years, Fracktal was a product company. We built 3D printers — good ones — pouring energy into better machines, better materials, better print quality, and assumed customers would find valuable applications on their own.

They didn't. The product was never the real bottleneck; the application was. Most customers who buy a 3D printer don't know what to print, how to design for it, or how to integrate it into their workflow. We were selling capability and hoping for adoption. Meanwhile, genuinely high-value problems — surgical planning, discontinued spare parts, manufacturing tooling — sat unsolved. Not because the technology wasn't ready, but because nobody was treating them as problems worth solving.

The thirteen years were not wasted. They produced manufacturing expertise, materials knowledge, and machine-building capability that very few teams in India possess. But the next chapter must be different: build solutions to specific, painful problems, where the machines and software we've developed become tools rather than the product itself.

I'll be direct about what this means for me personally. For thirteen years I have been, fundamentally, an operator — a good one, and a strong engineer. I built things. I improved things. I shipped things. What I didn't do well enough was step back and ask whether I was solving the right problems for the right customers at the right price. That's the shift I'm making now — reorienting everything around problem identification, commercial viability, and selling valuable outcomes rather than selling technology.

## What We've Built Underneath

Over the past year, we've gone AI-native across the entire company — every workflow from HR to sales to accounting now runs on agentic AI systems. A two-person software development team, working with AI agents, built what would normally require an engineering team many times that size. The team is capable. What we've lacked is clarity on where to aim that capability for the best commercial return.

The clearest insight came from watching our industrial customers up close. We have printers on the shop floors at Honda and Toyoda Gosei. We watched them spend weeks getting simple manufacturing fixtures — custom holding tools — designed and delivered through traditional suppliers. The first version often fails. The cycle repeats. The bottleneck is never manufacturing; it's the design decisions before manufacturing. That's where all the time and money go.

We built software that compresses that cycle from 3–4 hours to 10–15 minutes — under validation with Honda and Toyoda Gosei now. But more importantly, the underlying platform isn't specific to fixtures. It encodes 13 years of manufacturing domain knowledge into automated design workflows. The same engine that designs a fixture for Honda can, with the right adaptation, process a surgeon's CT scan into a patient-specific implant or reconstruct a discontinued spare part from a physical sample. The question isn't whether the technology works — it does. The question is which problems are worth building a business around.

## Two Directions Worth Exploring — Or Winding Down

So that's where we are. A capable team. Real technology. Proof points with serious customers. And an honest admission: the current business model is not working, and we don't yet know with certainty what should replace it.

I see two directions that could become commercially significant businesses — one in medical, one in manufacturing services. Both build on what we've spent 13 years learning. But both are hypotheses, not certainties. The purpose of this letter is to lay them out, explain why I believe they're worth a rigorous, time-bounded exploration, and ask for your help — both in deciding where to focus, and in being honest with me if neither is strong enough to justify continuing. If the answer is that our time and expertise are better invested elsewhere, I'd rather know that sooner than later.

## Direction 1: Scan-to-Surgery — Automated Surgical Planning & Patient-Specific Devices

We've completed 50+ surgical planning cases at Command Hospital Bangalore — primarily orthopedic: anatomical models for complex fractures, pre-surgical rehearsal planning, and custom implants. The approach extends to craniofacial reconstruction, spinal surgery, maxillofacial trauma, and tumour resection — anywhere patient-specific anatomy has surgical value.

All of it was done manually, using Materialise software, InVesalius, and 3D Slicer. Clinical results were excellent. But the process is too slow and too costly to scale.

This is precisely the workflow that Materialise and 3D Systems have turned into large businesses. Materialise's medical division did €134 million in FY2025, growing 15%. 3D Systems healthcare does ~$180 million annually. Globally, 3D-printed medical implants are a $2.8 billion market today, projected to reach $12 billion by 2034. In India, the market was $54 million in 2022 and is expected to reach $222 million by 2030.

Indian players are small and entirely manual — Jajal Medical (₹4.77 crore, FY2025), Anatomiz3D (₹1.86 crore, FY2024), Osteo3D, Graft3D. No one is automating the pipeline.

We're building that pipeline: an AI-automated workflow from DICOM scan to surgical plan to patient-specific device design. We've built the DICOM-to-3D viewer in-house. The planning automation engine is in development.

The critical distinction: we don't just design the devices — we build the printers that manufacture them. Our machines process PEEK (load-bearing orthopedic and spinal implants) and PCL and other bioresorbables through our work with Osteoforge (A Medical Materials Company). Vertical integration from scan to plan to printed implant — on hardware we designed ourselves — is rare globally and near-absent in India.

The same pipeline extends naturally into orthotics and prosthetics: braces, cranial helmets, prosthetic sockets, AFOs. Current fabrication is manual, slow, and craftsman-dependent. Automating it via 3D scanning and AI-generated design opens a large adjacent market — globally ~$7.5 billion. In India, over 2.5 crore people live with physical disabilities; the vast majority have no access to properly fitted assistive devices.

The broader point — and this connects to the mistake I described earlier — is that the most valuable medical businesses built on 3D printing don't sell 3D printing. They sell outcomes. Align Technology (Invisalign) does $4 billion a year selling clear aligners. Toothsi, India's equivalent, built a ₹100+ crore business. Both companies run fleets of 3D printers as a core part of their manufacturing workflow — but the patient never interacts with the printer. They interact with the result: straighter teeth, a better smile. The 3D printer is invisible infrastructure, not the product. If the Manipal ecosystem sees value in clear aligners — and with 49 hospitals, dental colleges, and clinics, the distribution is already there — we have the machines, the materials expertise, and the software capability to help stand that up. It could serve as a concrete, near-term application while the deeper surgical planning platform matures.

I don't want to repeat the old mistake — fall in love with the solution before finding the problem. I understand some Manipal institutions have begun exploring 3D printing for clinical applications, which suggests demand exists within the network. But whether scaling that capability is the most valuable problem they face, I genuinely don't know. What I'd like is a real conversation with the right people in the Manipal ecosystem — whoever you think would be most relevant on the clinical and medtech strategy side — before assuming what to build. Maybe it's surgical planning at scale across 49 hospitals. Maybe it's O&P. Maybe it's repurposing our DICOM-to-volume segmentation capability into broader medical imaging analysis tools. I'd rather discover the right problem than arrive with a pitch.

## Direction 2: The Digital Warehouse

The biggest barrier to 3D printing adoption in India isn't manufacturing capacity — customers don't know how to use the technology. They can't design for it, can't navigate process selection, can't get from a broken part or a rough sketch to a production-ready file. We've been waiting for them to figure this out. They haven't.

The shift: stop selling technology, start selling outcomes. Customer brings a problem — discontinued spare part, tooling bottleneck, legacy component — we deliver the finished part. Better turnaround, better pricing, no design expertise required on their end. The generative modeling, computational geometry, 3D printing, and vacuum casting are our internal machinery. They get their part.

What separates this from a job shop is the intelligence layer underneath. The real bottleneck in custom manufacturing isn't manufacturing — it's the pre-manufacturing decisions: reverse-engineering a broken sample, selecting the right process for a given geometry and material, designing for manufacturability, choosing materials. These require years of expertise and are why small-batch requests take weeks to quote and months to deliver.

We're encoding 13 years of manufacturing knowledge into software that automates these decisions. The cost structure is the important part: the intelligence runs at near-zero marginal cost per job once built. Thousands of jobs on the same infrastructure as ten. The moat isn't machines — those can be bought. It's the accumulated domain knowledge encoded into the system, which makes the pipeline scalable without linearly growing the team.

Vacuum casting is also critical to this story, and worth explaining briefly. There's a manufacturing gap nobody serves well: 3D printing is excellent for one-off prototypes but limited in material strength; injection molding produces production-quality parts but only makes economic sense above 5,000 units because the steel tooling costs lakhs. Between those two — the 10 to 500 unit range — there's vacuum casting. The process uses a 3D-printed master pattern to create a silicone mold, then pours production-grade polymer (functionally equivalent to injection-molded plastic) into that mold under vacuum. The result is real, usable parts — not prototypes — at a fraction of the tooling cost and time. It's the natural extension of 3D printing into small-batch production.

The problem is that vacuum casting has traditionally been a craftsman's art — manual, skill-dependent, high failure rates. We've automated the mold design and process parameter optimisation so that it works right the first time. Toyota doesn't need 50,000 bumper clips — they need 200, in the right polymer, on demand. Vacuum casting is the only process that works economically at that volume, and our automation makes it scalable across hundreds of part numbers.

The numbers: Toyoda Gosei — a Tier 1 Toyota Group supplier of rubber and plastic automotive components, with 67 group companies across 17 countries and plants across South India supplying Toyota, Maruti, Nissan, and Renault — told us directly they lose approximately ₹20 crore a year at a single plant on spare parts for discontinued vehicles. That's one company, one problem. Every major automotive OEM has versions of it. When a single customer can quantify a pain point at that scale, you're looking at the kind of concentrated, high-value problem worth building a company around.

This is a manufacturing platform play. Zetwerk (₹13,000 crore revenue) and Xometry ($630 million, 30% YoY growth) have proven the model at scale. Our focus is narrower — polymer manufacturing where 3D printing, vacuum casting, and domain-specific software intersect — but the playbook is well-proven. The question is whether we can land two or three anchor customers at the Toyota scale and prove the unit economics before we try to scale it.

I believe both directions described above are potentially large and valuable problems — the kind worth building a company around. But I'm not certain they're the _right_ problems for us to pursue, or that I'm seeing them clearly enough. That's where I need your help.

If I'm honest about where I lean: it's medical. With MEMG's hospital and education network behind us, we have access no other small additive manufacturing company in India has. My own father was an Army doctor — medicine isn't foreign to me, and our 50+ surgical planning cases show we can operate in this domain. But I want to be challenged on this. If the digital warehouse is the stronger bet, or if there's a path I'm not seeing, I'd rather hear that now.

I'd welcome time with anyone on your teams — market intelligence, sector experts, portfolio advisors, medtech leads — who could pressure-test these directions and help us understand where we'd add the most value. Happy to present to whoever you think is the right audience.

Specific introductions would make a real difference. Medical side: the right people in the Manipal ecosystem — whoever you think we should be talking to — so we can listen before we build. Manufacturing side: companies struggling with spare parts or small-batch production. Toyoda Gosei validates the problem at scale; we need two or three more anchor accounts to prove the model.

This pivot requires seed capital — not a large amount, but enough to run pilots and validate commercial scale.

If neither direction produces a viable path to scale, the right answer is to wind down — or find the right home for what we've built. Machine tool groups like ACE Micromatic, or industrial conglomerates building additive manufacturing capabilities, may see strategic value in our IP, our SLS expertise, and our defence and aerospace relationships. An acquisition that puts our technology into a larger distribution framework could be a better outcome than winding down to zero. I'm open to exploring that path if it's the right one. But I won't keep going on momentum alone.

A defined period. Defined capital. Your guidance on where to aim — and where not to.
