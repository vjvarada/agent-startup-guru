## THE CONVERSATION

### How It Went

Namala dominated the conversation. This was not a back-and-forth — it was a **transmission**. He talked for nearly 4 hours about his philosophy, his operating system, how he built Porus Labs, how he thinks about business. He **deliberately did not ask about my products or what I'm currently working on**. He told me:

> _"Are you solving the RIGHT problems?"_

> _"Don't get too much into the product itself, but look at high-value problems that customers have. Once you understand those high-value problems, raising money is very easy."_

This was not an evaluation meeting. This was a teaching conversation. He was giving me his playbook.

---

### NAMALA'S OPERATING SYSTEM

#### 1. Go After the Largest Fish

His defining strategy at Porus Labs was going after **GE Plastics** — the single largest, most valuable customer in his space — instead of spreading across many smaller accounts. He concentrated everything on winning that one whale.

**Lesson**: Don't spray your effort across 25 beta organizations at $1,500/seat. Find the ONE customer with the most expensive problem and solve it completely.

#### 2. Personally Handle Business Development

Namala personally owned BD, sales, proposals, and contracts at Porus. He didn't delegate the customer relationship. He was in the room, reading the people, understanding what they needed.

**Lesson**: The founder must be the one in front of the customer. Especially at this stage.

#### 3. Optimize for Concentrated, High-Value Customers

Rather than building a large customer base, Namala optimized for a small number of extremely high-value accounts. The result? Maximum revenue with minimum operational complexity. He had time to play golf.

**Lesson**: Revenue per customer matters more than number of customers. A few Rs 2-5 crore contracts beat 100 accounts at Rs 1 lakh each.

#### 4. Read People and Create Win-Win

Namala has an exceptional ability to **read people** — understand their motivations, what makes them succeed in their organization, what they actually need (vs. what they say they need). He described his approach as "soft manipulation" — but in service of creating outcomes where everyone wins.

He was transparent about this: by building this mentorship relationship with me, he said I am now **obligated to bring him any big opportunity I find**. This wasn't hidden — it was stated openly.

**Lesson**: Understand the human dynamics of any deal. Every person in a company has their own success metrics. Align your offering with what makes THEM look good.

#### 5. The Four Quotients

Namala believes what separates successful entrepreneurs from others is a combination of four quotients:

| Quotient             | What It Means                                                     |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **IQ**               | Intellectual capacity — can you understand complex problems?      |
| **EQ**               | Emotional intelligence — can you manage yourself and read others? |
| **Social Quotient**  | Network intelligence — can you build and leverage relationships?  |
| **Stress Tolerance** | Can you make good decisions under sustained, extreme pressure?    |

All four are required. Technical brilliance (IQ) alone is not enough. Sales ability (EQ + SQ) without stress tolerance will collapse under pressure.

#### 6. Big Industries to Watch

Namala mentioned three mega-sectors he's watching:

- **Energy**
- **AI**
- **Space**

He believes wealth accumulation at the top needs redistribution through investment — which is why he angel invests prolifically.

## ADDITIONAL NOTES — Recalled March 8, 2026

### Operator vs. Entrepreneur

This was one of the more significant threads of the conversation. Namala was deliberate about drawing a hard distinction between two types of people:

**The Operator**: Brilliant at execution. Figures out the solution, drives R&D, hires smart people, gets things built. Extremely capable — but fundamentally reactive: given a problem, they solve it exceptionally well.

**The Entrepreneur**: Identifies the right problem before anyone else does. Understands how to sell it, to whom, and at what price. Figures out who should execute it and how to get that execution out of people. Not necessarily the smartest person in the room — but the one who defines the room.

His point was not that operators are less valuable. His point was that **by default, all entrepreneurs are great operators, but not all great operators are entrepreneurs.** The mindset for entrepreneurship — problem identification, market framing, sales instinct, people orchestration — is rare and hard to develop. It's a fundamentally different cognitive mode from excellent execution.

He wanted me to understand which one I am, or which one I am becoming.

---

### My Own Reflection

By the end of the meeting, I came to a conclusion I've been circling for a long time: **my entrepreneurship journey, in the real sense of what Namala means, is just beginning.**

For thirteen years, I have been an exceptional operator. A great engineer. Someone who can build things — complex things — with discipline and technical depth. Fracktal Works was largely an operator's achievement: build better machines, improve materials, ship product. That's what I did, and I did it well.

What I haven't fully developed — what is now the challenge — is the entrepreneur's instinct: identifying the right problem, positioning the solution for the right buyer, orchestrating the right people to execute it. Not doing it myself. Making it happen through others and through market insight.

This is the frame I need to carry going into the next chapter. Less engineer. More entrepreneur.



### On Company Structure

At the end of the meeting, I briefly touched on what I'm currently building — Applied Additive / Rapid Tools — without going into detail. Namala's advice was clear:

**Do not spin it out as a separate company. Do it as a daughter company of Fracktal Works.**

His reasoning: structuring it as a subsidiary lets you restructure the cap table and fundraising for the new entity independently, while still taking care of existing investors. He was explicit about not leaving Fracktal behind — don't start something new and walk away. Keep existing investors (Mohandas Pai, Ranjan Pai) in the picture and on the ride. The subtext is clearly about preserving those relationships rather than burning them by starting fresh elsewhere.

**Key takeaway**: Daughter company structure. Fracktal Works as parent. Applied Additive as subsidiary with its own cap table and fundraising path.

### His Caution on Medical Devices

Namala was somewhat skeptical about the medical direction. His concerns, briefly stated:

- Medical devices can be too difficult to sell
- May be too niche a market
- Requires complex, multi-layered distribution channels
- The path from product to revenue is unclear

Most importantly: **it doesn't fit his core philosophy** — one customer, large and recurring returns. Medical, with its lengthy sales cycles, regulatory complexity, and hospital-by-hospital adoption, doesn't match the "single whale customer" model he champions.

This is worth weighing seriously. Namala isn't a medical skeptic — he co-founded a biotech (Avammune). But he's skeptical of it as a go-to-market for a small team with limited capital.

### Industries He Believes Will Win

On a high level, Namala identified three sectors he believes are on an irreversible upward trajectory:

1. **Energy** — Clean energy transition, grid infrastructure, new energy sources
2. **AI** — Across all applications; the platform shift is real
3. **Space** — Long-run play; will take time but the opportunity is enormous

Worth holding these as orientation points. Manufacturing AI sits at the intersection of #1 (energy efficiency, industrial output) and #2, which may be part of what makes the Digital Warehouse direction interesting to someone like him.
